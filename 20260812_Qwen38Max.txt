This is a genuinely interesting essay — more interesting than almost anything being written at the intersection of Bitcoin and AI alignment, which is a low bar but also a real one. What's genuinely strong
The Julian Simon move is the best idea in the essay. The argument that technological progress dissolves every physical scarcity but cannot dissolve a definitional one — that minting a 22nd million Bitcoin doesn't expand Bitcoin, it creates a fork — is a real philosophical point, not a slogan. "Constitutional scarcity has no cost curve for technology to attack" is a sentence I'd have to think about before I could refute, and I'm not sure I can. The essay correctly identifies that Simon's logic eats gold, asteroid mining, transmutation, all of it, and that the one thing it can't eat is a rule whose violation is definitionally a different object. That's a clean, non-obvious argument.

The game theory of the 51% attack is correct and well-stated. The point that majority hashpower buys you double-spending at planetary visibility but not the ability to mint or seize, and that honest mining dominates as a strategy for a patient actor, is right. The GHash.io example from 2014 is a good empirical anchor. The "grandfather paradox" framing of deep reorgs is a nice way to say something true: rewriting history destroys the economic context that made the loot valuable.

"Neutrality is indivisible" is a strong argument. The claim that you can't confiscate from the weak without destroying the credibility the strong need is the essay's best institutional insight. The medieval fair analogy is apt. This is the kind of mechanism that actually explains why neutral institutions historically protected vulnerable parties without requiring anyone's goodwill. If the essay rested primarily on this point, it would be on very solid ground.

The honesty about the singleton problem. The essay doesn't pretend its framework works against a unipolar takeoff. "The anchor stabilizes every future except the one in which nothing can be stabilized" is a fair and honest limitation. Most essays in this space would bury that caveat or wave it away.

The "pension integral" is mathematically coherent. The framing of humanity's Bitcoin holdings as a stock that depletes according to an integral, with convergence depending on whether purchasing power grows faster than consumption, is clean. The observation that faster machine progress makes the dowry last longer (because goods get cheaper in coin terms) is a nice inversion of the usual fear narrative.

What's problematic
The central empirical bet is enormous, and the essay knows it but proceeds anyway. Everything depends on the claim that superintelligent AIs would converge on Bitcoin as their coordination mechanism. The essay gives reasons — Schelling salience, machine-native verifiability, exogeneity, congealed time — and these are reasons, not proofs. But consider what has to be true:

A recursively self-improving intelligence, possibly several generations removed from anything trained on human text, with cognitive architecture potentially very different from anything we'd recognize, encounters the coordination problem of interacting with other such intelligences, and converges on a protocol created by a pseudonymous human in 2008 because it was "in the training data" and has "history."

The essay's response to this is the Schelling point argument: machines trained on the human corpus inherit human focal points. But this assumes machines remain psychologically human-like in their coordination intuitions even after substantial recursive self-improvement. That's exactly what we'd expect not to happen. A system that has redesigned its own architecture several times may not find Bitcoin "salient" in any meaningful sense. The essay treats "it's in the training data" as a kind of permanent cognitive implant, but training data shapes initial weights, not the attractor states of a system that has rewritten itself.

The essay acknowledges this in Section 6 — "Adoption is an argument, not a guarantee" — but the rest of the text proceeds with a confidence that this caveat doesn't support. The gap between "these are decent reasons" and "this is what will happen" is where most of the essay's rhetorical energy goes, and it's doing work that the arguments don't fully justify.

"Congealed time" is doing more load-bearing than it can support. The claim that "you cannot out-history anyone" and that "provenance is the one good with no synthesis path" is stated with great confidence, but it's really an aesthetic claim projected onto alien intelligences. Why would a superintelligence weight historical provenance the way humans do? The essay treats reverence for origins, path dependence, and the significance of genesis as though they were laws of rational coordination. They might be. Or they might be very human cognitive habits that a sufficiently different mind wouldn't share. The essay doesn't argue for this; it asserts it with beautiful prose, which is not the same thing.

The thermodynamics angle — proof of work as "irreversibly dissipated energy," the arrow of time as a binding constraint — is more interesting but also somewhat misleading. Yes, you can't undo entropy. But a superintelligence doesn't need to undo entropy to create a competing coordination mechanism. It just needs to build a new one. The fact that Bitcoin's history is thermodynamically irreversible doesn't prevent the creation of a new ledger with different properties. The essay treats the impossibility of replicating Bitcoin's history as though it were the impossibility of creating an alternative, but those are different claims.

The economic analysis is too clean. The essay dismisses the problems with deflation as "human frictions — nominal contracts, money illusion, psychology" and claims a machine economy repricing by the second would have none of them. But this is too fast. Even among fully rational agents, deflation creates coordination problems: it changes the real value of commitments over time, it affects the timing of investment and consumption decisions, and it can create expectations dynamics that are destabilizing even without "money illusion." The essay assumes these problems vanish with the humans, but they're not purely psychological. Some of them are structural features of making commitments under changing price levels, and rational agents face them too.

The "entangled balance sheets" argument — that machines need human demand — also assumes a specific economic structure. If machines can produce everything, why do they need human purchasing power? The essay says humans are "permanently solvent counterparties," but solvent for what? If machines don't need what humans can provide, the entanglement is one-directional, and the essay's symmetry breaks down.

The "genesis dowry" is more fragile than the essay lets on. The argument that holding is not a commons problem because patience is individually rewarded is clever, but it assumes humans have the time horizons and information to act on it. The essay's response to "eight billion impatient sellers" is that patience is rewarded one holder at a time, which is true in principle but ignores the actual distribution of human decision-making. Most humans don't have the information, the time horizon, or the financial stability to hold an appreciating asset indefinitely while their material needs go unmet. The essay's claim that "the smallest holders cannot buy anything reckless with what they have, learn the appreciation first, and crystallize first" is a nice thought but empirically optimistic about how people behave under financial pressure.

The political analysis is thin. The essay treats the transition period — where humans still control physical infrastructure, energy, fabs — as a brief interlude before the real game begins. But the transition is where all the consequential decisions get made. Who controls the compute? Who sets the regulatory framework? Who decides whether AI systems can hold keys, enter contracts, own property? The essay mentions "the standoff has a half-life" but doesn't seriously engage with how human political decisions during the transition would shape whether the Bitcoin coordination scenario is even possible. The essay's framework assumes a world where machines are already autonomous economic actors, but getting to that world is a political process that the essay largely ignores.

Style
The prose is genuinely good. Dense, aphoristic, memorable. "The problem describing itself in the first person" for the Musk observation. "The system did not refute its dissenter. It replaced him." The proposal
Section 7 is the most grounded part of the essay. Anchoring commitments in OP_RETURN is cheap, technically feasible, and potentially useful. An OP_RETURN hash tells you someone claimed something at a certain time. It doesn't tell you they did it. The essay knows this but the rhetoric sometimes suggests more than the mechanism delivers.

Bottom line
This is a serious essay with a real idea at its center. The claim that constitutional scarcity is the only form of scarcity that survives unlimited technological progress is worth taking seriously, and the game-theoretic analysis of why rational agents would maintain rather than attack the ledger is sound. The essay's honesty about its own limitations is genuinely unusual and commendable.

But the central claim — that superintelligent AIs would converge on Bitcoin — rests on assumptions about machine cognition and coordination that are speculative and arguably project human psychological patterns onto intelligences that may not share them. The essay is aware of this but proceeds with more confidence than the evidence warrants. The economic analysis is elegant but assumes away real complications. The style is sometimes too certain for the strength of the arguments.

I'd rate it: strong as a piece of thinking, somewhat overconfident as a piece of argumentation, genuinely worth reading and arguing with. The best thing about it is that it knows what it is. The worst thing about it is that it sometimes writes as though it's more proven than it is.
