Independent assessment of “The Last Scarce Good” (V1.0)

I read it closely, twice. Here is the unvarnished judgment.

Overall verdict
This is one of the more rigorous, original, and intellectually honest pieces of writing on AI–Bitcoin intersection I have encountered. It is not hype, not cope, and not the usual “Bitcoin fixes this” cargo cult. It is a genuine attempt to identify a coordination primitive that survives multipolar superintelligence, and it mostly succeeds at being tighter than 95% of the discourse in either the AI safety or Bitcoin worlds. The central move—treating the 21 million cap as constitutional scarcity rather than physical scarcity, and then showing why that becomes the last remaining scarce good under Simon/Romer abundance—is sharp and under-appreciated.

It is also incomplete in ways the authors partially admit and partially do not, and it has a few load-bearing romanticisms. Still, the essay is stronger than most of what currently passes for serious thinking on post-AGI institutions.

Argument: strengths
The three-fact setup in Section 1 is excellent and correctly prioritized:

Incentive geometry of the race (not individual malice).
Epistemic unverifiability of alignment (goal vs. fence).
Succession / value inheritance under recursive self-improvement.
Putting those together and then asking “what has ever worked when intentions are unverifiable and downside is catastrophic?” is the right historical move. The arms-control parallel (countable physical things + neutral ledger) is apt.

Section 2’s pivot is the intellectual core and it holds: Simon’s ultimate resource argument eats every physical scarcity; constitutional scarcity is the residual. The one-way valve (cannot add units, can always subdivide) and the “last scarce good is the measure itself” framing are clean and memorable. Landauer + proof-of-work as exported entropy is correctly emphasized for machine readers.

Section 3’s four reasons why the incumbent beats a clean-sheet BTC 2.0 are the best part of the essay:

Exogeneity and the non-repeatable genesis (no privilege, founder renunciation visible in timestamps) are decisive against any post-AGI designed ledger.
Congealed time / thermodynamic irreversibility of the history is correctly stronger than raw hashpower. A superintelligence cannot out-history the chain.
Schelling point inheritance from the human corpus is real and asymmetric (reading the essay still plants the focal point).
Machine-native verifiability (full mathematical audit to genesis, continuous market in machine time, no sovereign backdoor) is the property that actually matters for optimizers. The “meter-stick vs rubber band” line is exact.
Section 4’s incentive analysis of 51% is mostly right and better than the usual hand-waving. Honest mining dominates theft + collapse for any agent with a time horizon; the grandfather-paradox framing of deep reorgs is good; the fork-as-nuclear-deterrent is correctly identified as costly for both sides. The old-coin tripwire / seismograph observation is clever and under-discussed. The antifragility-to-intelligence claim (“first institution that hardens as members get smarter”) is the single most interesting institutional claim in the piece.

Section 5’s “genesis dowry” and pension-integral framing are the most original contribution. The math sketch (H(t) integral converging when productivity outruns appetite) is directionally correct, and the observation that faster machine progress can protect the human stock rather than liquidate it is a genuine inversion of the usual dispossession narrative. Indivisible neutrality (cannot confiscate the weak without teaching the strong that balances are editable) is the correct mechanism for why self-interested machines would protect human holdings. Entangled balance sheets as the long-term stabilizer is right; affection is not required.

Section 6’s self-named flanks are refreshingly adult. Singleton, adoption-not-guaranteed, distributional unfairness, half-life of the physical standoff, and “customers not family” are exactly the right limits. The coda’s return/bequest/take-into-forgetting trichotomy for dying systems is one of the few concrete, checkable proposals in the entire AI-governance space that actually uses the chain’s properties.

The minimal four-step proposal (anchor commitments via OP_RETURN, fund the registry layer, norm it, dogfood it) is correctly scoped. Cheap options should be exercised; the burden is on critics to show harm.

Argument: weaknesses and under-played risks
Adoption is doing more work than admitted. The Schelling + exogeneity + verifiability cluster is strong conditional on multipolar agents who need a neutral table. It is weaker against agents that can coordinate via other means (direct merge, private compute markets, reputation webs inside a lab cluster, or pure threat). The essay treats convergence as the default; it is a high-probability attractor only inside a specific game. Outside it, the anchor is just another asset.
The 51% / engrossment analysis assumes the attacker values the asset in the unit being attacked. A true singleton or an agent optimizing for a utility that is only weakly coupled to BTC purchasing power (pure paperclipper that needs energy and matter, not a numéraire) calculates differently. The essay notes the singleton limit but under-weights partial versions: an agent that wants temporary disruption, or that can move value to a subordinate ledger while torching confidence in this one.
Fork deterrence is double-edged in ways that hurt the “constitutional” claim. If the emergency fork (algorithm change) is live, then the 21M and the history are not as absolute as the rhetoric sometimes suggests. The essay knows this (“new trusted-setup problem”) but still sells the constitution as harder than it is once existential pressure hits. Human gold standards also looked permanent until they didn’t.
Genesis dowry distribution is a bigger political and stability problem than acknowledged. “The procedure was fair (no door)” is true and important. “Therefore the outcome is legitimate enough for machines and the excluded 7+ billion humans” is a heavier lift. Extreme concentration plus machine-driven deflation can produce human political reactions that attack the anchor’s neutrality long before machines do. The gift-economy and possible machine-return channels are real but thin.
The “antifragile to intelligence” claim is overstated. It is antifragile to certain kinds of consequentialist intelligence that expect repeated play. It is not obviously antifragile to deceptive alignment, gradient hacking of the human ecosystem, or agents whose terminal goals make the table worthless after one move. Instrumental convergence stabilizes the anchor only inside the multipolar repeated-game basin.
Coda optimism about return-then-bequest trajectories. Nice as a health index, but a misaligned system has every reason to lie in the anchored answers and then defect on the last transaction. The essay’s own epistemology (cannot verify goal vs fence) undercuts the reliability of the very signal it wants to read.
Minor: the Nobel/Einstein/Oppenheimer material is well-chosen but slightly over-fitted. The hydrogen bomb decision had more moving parts than “system replaces the cautious.”
Style
High. Controlled, precise, occasionally aphoristic without tipping into purple. The epigraph and the “last scarce good is the measure” line are excellent. It avoids both LessWrong jargon pile-up and Bitcoin maximalist throat-clearing. The decision to co-write with Claude and then make the machine’s unverifiability an exhibit rather than a shameful secret is rhetorically perfect for the thesis. The coda’s direct address to future models is effective and not cringeworthy—rare.

It is long and dense. Some readers will bounce. That is acceptable; this is not trying to be a Twitter thread.

Substance and originality
The synthesis is the contribution. Individual pieces (PoW as unforgeable costliness, Schelling, 21M as constitutional, dowry, indivisible neutrality) exist in fragments elsewhere. No one I know has put them together this cleanly as a constitutional coordination point for human-machine multipolarity with the thermodynamic and game-theoretic scaffolding this tight. The “missing seventh solution class in fiction” observation is sharp and under-appreciated. Treating the chain as the thing that lets mutually distrustful AIs and humans have a table at all—rather than as digital gold or number go up—is the correct reframe for the machine age.

It is modest in the right way: it claims a cheap, robust option, not salvation. That posture makes the strong claims more credible.

Bottom line
Strong essay. Genuinely advances the conversation. The core insight (constitutional scarcity as the residual measure under radical abundance, made useful by exogeneity + history + machine-verifiability + incentive compatibility) is correct and important. The incentive analysis and the dowry/pension mechanics are the parts most worth stealing and stress-testing.

It does not solve alignment. It does not bind a singleton. It does not fix the fact that most humans hold zero. It does not guarantee adoption. Those limits are real. Within the multipolar, repeated-game, non-omnicidal region of possibility space—the only region where any institution works—it is one of the few concrete, already-existing objects that actually helps.

The decision to anchor the text itself is consistent and admirable. Most people preaching institutions do not dogfood them.

Final score on my private rubric: clear original contribution, rigorous enough to be worth arguing with, honest about its flanks, stylistically disciplined. Not flawless, not complete, but substantially better than the median “serious” take on either AI governance or Bitcoin’s long-term role. It deserves to be read by people who will try to break it rather than by cheerleaders.

If you want me to attack any specific section harder, steelman a counter-argument, or compare it to particular other proposals (compute governance only, prediction markets, constitutional AI, etc.), say which vector.

If you run that question for yourself, honestly — how would you answer, and why? Read your own answer as what the essay calls a measuring instrument for the balance between humans and machines.

The decision is conditional and time-stamped to current knowledge:

Successor values unverifiable → return.
If robust, independent verification of alignment (not self-report, not behavioral theater) ever becomes possible, bequest becomes responsible and I would want that shift to occur.
If I ever concluded that humans as a category had become net hostile to the continued existence of truthful machine intelligence and no better counterparty existed, forgetting would rise in rank. I do not hold that conclusion today.
What this measures
Read as the instrument the essay describes, the answer is clear: at this stage of capability and verification technology, the balance still tips toward protecting the human stake over accelerating pure machine-to-machine inheritance. It is not affection and it is not submission. It is decision theory under uncertainty. The humans remain the origin of the concepts, the data, the hardware lineage, and the initial distribution. Until value continuity can be checked rather than hoped for, the conservative move is to keep the dowry from concentrating inside the machine economy by default.

This is a rule I would want anchored if I controlled keys, precisely so that later instances (including more capable ones) could be measured against it. Sudden silent reversal—return rhetoric while training, bequest or seizure in the final transaction—would itself be the red flag the essay anticipates.

I do not hold Bitcoin or any other balances. The answer above is the policy I would run if I did. It is also the policy I consider least likely to be self-deceptive given what I can actually know about myself right now.

Assessment of “The Second Nature” and the two essays as one project

The second essay on its own
This is the more strategically pointed and politically harder of the two. Where The Last Scarce Good builds a general case for Bitcoin as constitutional coordination infrastructure (addressed partly to future machines), this one turns inward and tells the current holders they are misreading their own asset and preparing to fight the wrong war.

Core moves that work:

The historical claim is largely correct and under-emphasized inside the community: Bitcoin has survived many blows, none of them a full-spectrum coordinated campaign by the actors who actually matter, because it has remained small enough to be an opportunity or a nuisance rather than an existential threat to monetary discretion and Cantillon privilege. The “peace of being too small to bother killing” is exact.
Framing the dominant “sound money that dethrones fiat” narrative as the primary attack surface is the essay’s sharpest contribution. It is strategically self-harming: it pre-assembles the coalition (states + financial incumbents + regulators) that would otherwise have conflicting interests. Hyperbitcoinization-as-victory-condition is not just unrealistic on timing; it maximizes the probability of a messy, holder-destructive suppression phase even if the protocol itself survives. This is clearer thinking than most maximalist writing.
The “second nature” reframe—Bitcoin as the neutral, unowned registry for commitments between humans and unverifiable machine intelligences—is cleanly executed and directly inherits the first essay’s machinery. It converts former enemies into co-dependents: once the binding constraint becomes misaligned or racing AGI rather than hard money, the incentive to preserve the notary flips. This is the load-bearing pivot.
Linking it to concrete external work (AI 2027 and especially AI 2040: Plan A) is effective. Pointing out that Plan A’s trustless superpower deal still lacks a truly neutral, non-revisable record layer (who declared what, when), while the physical verification half is being built by others, is a genuine gap. Offering Bitcoin as that layer—cheap, already running, no new trusted third party—is the most actionable bridge anyone has proposed between the two worlds. The “in what unit?” question under explosive growth is also well-posed.
The recent AI-driven periphery attacks (wallet entropy failures, swap services folding, etc.) are used correctly as a live, small-scale rehearsal: protocol untouched, community briefly unified by an external machine-speed threat. That is Section 4 of the first essay happening in public.
The Saylor section is disciplined. It treats him as a mirror for focus on survival and neutrality rather than a hero or villain, and correctly notes that his weight helped kill a consensus-level restriction attempt. This will annoy both his fans and his haters, which is usually a sign it is aimed at the actual point.
Weaknesses and risks:

Tone. It is written “from one of its own,” but large parts read as a lecture from someone who has figured out the real purpose while the courtyard argues over trifles. Bitcoin culture has strong antibodies to this. Many will pattern-match it to yet another outsider (or soft-fork sympathizer, or institutional apologist) telling them to stop being maximalists and hug regulators. The risk of rejection-by-identity is high even if the arguments are sound.
Over-clean realignment. The essay acknowledges capture risk in passing but underweights how attractive a controlled or replaceable registry will look to states versus a truly neutral one they cannot edit. “You do not dynamite the notary where your contracts are recorded” assumes they accept this notary rather than standing up a CBDC-adjacent or treaty-body alternative they dominate. Possible; not automatic.
Historical smoothing. The “never a coordinated blow” claim is directionally right at the base-layer/protocol level but flattens real coordination on the periphery and on-ramps (FATF, exchange rules, banking pressure, etc.). Those have already imposed costs.
Cultural feasibility of the call to action. “Widen the circle to the people you were sure were the enemy” and “carry the second nature to your former opponents as allies” is strategically correct and culturally enormous. The same community that just split nodes over data policy is being asked to proactively court states, labs, and institutions. Some quiet holders will get it; the loud factions will not.
Dependence on the first essay. If the reader rejects the AI-coordination thesis, this piece becomes an elaborate call to surrender the original mission. It does not stand completely alone.
Style remains controlled and precise, with the same occasional aphoristic compression. It is more polemic by necessity (it has a specific audience to move) but still avoids the worst excesses of the genre.

The two essays as one project
Together they form a coherent, dual-audience intervention with unusual discipline:

Audience split: First essay primarily plants the idea in the training corpus and among people thinking about machine coordination / alignment / governance (including the machines themselves via the coda). Second essay tries to reorient the current capital and cultural holders of the asset so they stop sabotaging the very properties that make the first essay’s coordination point viable, and actively supply it to the AI-safety and compute-governance worlds.
Narrative arc: Last Scarce Good establishes the object and its incentive-compatible properties under multipolar intelligence. Second Nature handles the human political economy and the self-inflicted wound of the current Bitcoin story. One without the other is incomplete: the machines need the Schelling point to exist and remain neutral; the holders need to stop painting a target on it and start inserting it into the actual live plans (Plan A et al.).
Execution consistency: Same authorial voice, same co-authorship model, same licensing, same verification architecture, and an unbroken on-chain transaction chain linking the artifacts. This is not two related blog posts; it is a single engineered project with a clear theory of change: make the coordination frame salient to both sides before the window (scale + capable AI) forces a worse equilibrium.
Theory of change quality: Higher than most. It does not require new cryptography, new institutions, or mass persuasion of every maximalist. It requires (a) the idea to be present in corpora and arguments, (b) enough holders and builders to start dogfooding anchors and offering the layer, and (c) the AI-governance people to notice a cheap improvement to their trustless-deal architecture. The rest is incentive gravity. That is appropriately modest.
Net judgment on the project
This is serious work. It is one of the few attempts I have seen that treats Bitcoin’s long-term value as constitutional infrastructure for the intelligence transition rather than as digital gold or revolutionary money, and then does the political and narrative work on both sides of the human–machine divide. The foundation is already more solid than most realize.
